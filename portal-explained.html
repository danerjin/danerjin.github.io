The whole system, in one page

Everything that follows is this, in more detail and with the receipts. If you read only one section, read this one.

The problem

Your camera sits above your screen while your friend's face is on the screen, so you are never looking where the camera is. A 27-inch monitor at arm's length puts that gap at 18.4°, and humans read gaze to about 1°. But the loss that matters isn't eye contact — it's the difference between someone looking at your left eye, your right eye, or your mouth. That's the channel carrying attention and reference, and it is entirely absent from every video call ever made.

Stated properly: a window transmits a 4D light field — where a ray crosses the glass, and which way it's heading. A camera keeps only the rays through one aperture, collapsing direction to a point, and a flat screen then re-emits that image the same in all directions. Direction is destroyed at capture and never restored at display. A video call doesn't have the camera in the wrong place; it transmits the wrong slice of a bigger thing.

The idea

Don't detect gaze — rebuild the window. Both ends agree on one rectangle W in millimetres. Each screen is that rectangle. Then two rules: draw the far person from your tracked eye position, through a frustum whose near plane is W; and hold 1:1 physical scale, never zooming to fill.

Do that and gaze is not corrected, it's never broken. Nothing in the pipeline knows what an eye is — which is why pointing, following someone's gaze past your shoulder, and leaning to see around something all work without being implemented.

The reduction that makes it affordable

You can't reproduce a 4D light field on a $200 panel. You don't have to. There is one viewer and their eye position is measurable, so you only need the single 2D slice converging on that eye. Which slice → head tracking. Getting the slice → depth and reprojection. Emitting it → nothing special at all, because once you know where the eye is, position on the screen encodes direction. Angular emission is only needed when you don't know where the viewer is — which is exactly what a $25,000 light-field display is buying.

How a frame actually gets from their face to your eye

  1. Nine fixed cameras watch them. Nothing moves, ever.
  2. Two independent depth solves — one over the face volume, one over the near volume — triangulate depth: every pixel that survives learns its distance in millimetres. The ones that don't survive get no depth and stay holes.
  3. Depth turns each pixel from a dot on a grid into a point in space — and points in space can be photographed from anywhere.
  4. Those points are reprojected to the viewpoint your eye occupies. Near pixels move further than far ones; that's parallax, falling out of the arithmetic.
  5. Gaps uncovered by the move are filled from whichever camera saw behind the edge — real pixels, not invented ones.
  6. The result is sent with its depth map, and your machine re-renders it from your live head position every frame. Network lag becomes conversational lag, never geometric error.

Every displayed pixel is a resampled sensor pixel from the current frame. No model is fitted, nothing persists between frames, and no machine learning touches the image path.

What it deliberately does not do

Five declared blind spots, listed in full in §21. They are decisions rather than bugs, and the right place for them is the product, not a support ticket.

  • Anything within about 150 mm of the glass has no depth. A hand thrust at the screen reads as a hole, not as a gesture.
  • One tracked viewer per end. A second person is looking at a picture drawn for somebody else's eye.
  • Head turns past roughly 60° leave usable stereo coverage.
  • Viewer travel past ±250 mm leaves the camera hull and the overscan margin together.
  • The render is monoscopic and focused at the screen. Direction is exact at every depth; stereopsis and accommodation are absent.

And ten ways it might still fail, in §22 — the first of which is that nobody has checked whether the matcher runs at frame rate, and in numpy it is four orders of magnitude away.

Cost is about $310 per end, before whatever the depth stage turns out to need. Both ends need a rig for gaze to work in both directions.

1 · The problem

ELI5

Your camera sits above your screen. Your friend's face is on the screen. So when you look at your friend, you're looking below the camera — and the camera films you looking down. You can never meet eyes. Every video call you have ever had, both people are looking slightly past each other, forever.

Put a number on it. Sit 600 mm from a 27-inch monitor with a webcam on the top bezel. The camera is about 190 mm above the point on the screen where the other person's eyes are drawn. That is an angle at your face of

alpha = 190 mm / 570 mm = 18.4°

Humans resolve gaze direction to roughly 1° horizontally. So 18.4° is not a subtle degradation. It is eighteen times the threshold.

But the real loss is finer than "eye contact"

The thing that matters is not whether you appear to be looking at the other person. It is the difference between looking at their left eye, their right eye, their mouth, their hands, or past their shoulder. That is the channel that carries attention, turn-taking, and reference.

Why vertical is cheaper than horizontal

Gaze sensitivity is asymmetric. A camera offset above the screen reads as mildly inattentive; the same offset to the side reads as actively avoiding you. This is why every mounting decision later in the design puts the offset vertically and never laterally.

2 · Why the obvious fix is wrong

Products already exist that "fix" this — NVIDIA Broadcast, FaceTime attention correction. They find your iris in the image and rotate it toward the camera.

ELI5

Imagine a filter that redraws your eyes so you're always staring straight ahead. Now you have perfect eye contact — all the time. Even when you glance at your notes. Even when you look at someone else. Even when you look away because you're thinking.

You didn't fix the channel. You jammed it on maximum and threw away everything it was carrying.

This is the crucial point and it is worth being precise about: gaze redirection is not an imperfect solution to this problem. It is a solution to a different problem. It optimises for "appears to be making eye contact", which is the one thing you would get for free anyway from the Mona Lisa effect (§15). It destroys the differential, which is the only thing that was hard.

3 · The reframe — a broken window

ELI5

Stop thinking of the screen as a television showing a picture of a person. Think of it as a hole in the wall with a person on the other side.

Through a real hole in a wall, eye contact is not a feature anyone had to implement. It just happens, because light travels in straight lines. If you rebuild the hole properly, you don't need to know what an eye is.

A video call is a window that has been broken in two specific ways: the viewpoint is wrong (it's at the camera, not at your eye), and the scale is wrong (the image is zoomed to fill the frame). Fix both and gaze is restored as a side effect.

What is actually being rebuilt

Stating this precisely makes everything downstream follow, so it is worth doing before anything else.

A window is a ray-preserving surface. Light crossing it arrives at a point heading in a direction, and leaves that same point still heading that way. What it transmits is the 4D light field: two dimensions of position on the glass, two of direction.

A camera destroys three of those four dimensions, and a screen fails to restore them. A camera keeps only the rays through one aperture — a 2D slice indexed by sensor position, with direction collapsed to a point. A flat panel then re-emits that image diffusely: every pixel the same colour in every direction. Direction is destroyed at capture and not reproduced at display.

So the honest framing is not "the camera is in the wrong place." A video call transmits a 2D slice where a window transmits a 4D field, and the slice it keeps is the wrong one.

The asymmetry that makes it tractable

You do not need the 4D field. There is one viewer, and their eye position is measurable. All that eye can receive is the bundle of rays converging on it — one 2D slice, chosen by where the eye is.

Every later decision spends its budget on that reduction: which slice is set by head tracking, getting the slice is set by depth and reprojection, and emitting it needs no directional display at all — because once the eye position is known, position on the screen encodes direction. Which pixel a ray leaves from, plus where the eye is, determines the ray completely. Angular emission is only necessary when you do not know where the viewer is.

That is why a dumb diffuse panel suffices, and it is the hinge of the whole design.

Why a camera is not a window

ELI5

A window lets a ray of light go straight through. It arrives at some point on the glass heading in some direction, and it leaves that same point still heading that way.

A camera does the opposite. It has one tiny hole, and the only light it keeps is the light that happens to pass through that hole. Every ray gets bent to a single point, and then the screen sprays it back out again from a different place. The path from their face to your eye has a kink in it.

That kink is the entire problem, and it is worth being exact about where it sits. Light leaves the far person's face in every direction. A window would pass the particular ray aimed at your eye straight through. A camera instead keeps only the ray aimed at its own aperture, and the display then re-emits that ray toward you as though it had come from your direction all along.

So the angle to measure is at the subject: between the ray a camera actually captures and the ray that should have reached your eye. That angle is alpha, and it is the same alpha the rest of this page is about.

What Portal actually does about it

It does not restore the light field. It cannot: a flat diffuse panel emits the same colour in every direction, so the directional information a window carries is gone twice over — destroyed once at the aperture, and not reproduced at the display. No amount of processing puts it back.

Instead it exploits the fact that there is only one viewer, and we know where their eye is. You do not need every ray. You need the single bundle of rays converging on that one eye — a 2D slice of the 4D light field. Three moves get you that slice:

  1. Put the virtual camera where your eye is. Not near the screen — at your actual eye position, mapped through the window into their room. A camera there captures precisely the image a window would have shown you, because every ray it collects is a ray that would have reached your eye.
  2. Synthesise it, since nothing can physically be there. That point is behind their screen. Depth tells you where every captured pixel sits in 3D, so you can re-project the real camera's pixels to the viewpoint you need. That is the warp — and it is why every output pixel must be a resampled input pixel rather than a model.
  3. Let position on the screen encode direction. The panel is diffuse, but that stops mattering once the viewer's eye is known: which pixel a ray leaves from, together with where the eye is, fully determines the ray. Draw the correct off-axis perspective at 1:1 and the geometry is exact. Directional emission is only needed when you don't know where the viewer is.
Why the displacement splits so unevenly

Moving the viewpoint from the real camera to your eye is a 596 mm pull-back plus a small sideways step. The pull-back is free — receding along the sight line reveals no surfaces that were hidden, so there is nothing to invent. The sideways step is the whole cost, because it does reveal new surfaces, and its size as an angle at the subject is alpha. This is why §7 decomposes the problem the way it does, and why the blade exists: it is a mechanical way to make the sideways step small before any processing happens.

Which also explains why the beamsplitter (§20) is optically superior and keeps getting mentioned. It puts a real camera at the conjugate point physically. There is no kink to correct, so there is no warp, no depth map, and no disocclusion — just raw video that is already right. Portal is what you build when you want that geometry without 90 cm of desk.

4 · The window contract

Both endpoints agree on one rectangle W, measured in millimetres. Each endpoint's display is W. Two rules:

  1. The far person's image is rendered from the near person's tracked eye position, through an asymmetric frustum whose near plane is W.
  2. Scale is 1:1 in physical units. Never zoom to fill.

The proof, in three lines

A fixates the pixel where B's eye appears on A's screen. By construction that pixel lies on the line from A's eye to B's virtual eye — that is what rule 1 means. So A's gaze ray is aimed at B in the shared frame. Render A from B's actual eye position, and B sees the ray arriving. The same argument holds for any target, which is why pointing works without being implemented.

The construction is Alberti's window

A painter stands where your eye is, holds a pane of glass at W, and traces what they see onto it. Step aside, put your eye where theirs was, and the tracing lines up with what is behind it. The eye-to-glass distance is used once — to decide where on the glass each point lands — and that same distance is what makes you see the drawn pixel in the right direction. It is not traversed twice.

And it is exact, not approximate. For a point P behind the glass, the ray from your eye E to P crosses W at S. Light the pixel at S and you see it along E→S, which is the ray E→P, because S lies on it. Checked numerically at 250 mm, 550 mm and 2000 mm behind the window: the angle between the true ray and the drawn pixel is zero to machine precision, and angular size matches a real window at every depth. There is no small-angle approximation anywhere in the contract.

What falls out of it

  • No chair needed. Head tracking replaces it.
  • Different seating distances are fine and self-consistent. If A sits 500 mm back and B sits 1300 mm back, they are 1800 mm apart in the shared frame and both views agree. It only breaks if you fake a distance to fill the frame.
  • The system is gaze-agnostic. Nothing anywhere in the pipeline knows what an eye is.

Realising the contract

The contract demands an image taken from a viewpoint no camera can occupy — your eye, on the far side of their window. Everything in this section is how that image is obtained, because this is the step most easily hand-waved and it is where the design either holds or does not.

The cameras never move

All five are rigidly bolted and must stay that way; the entire calibration assumes it, and §18 treats a bumped camera the way you would treat a bumped tripod mid-exposure. No motors, no gimbals, nothing to distract you. The camera that moves is virtual — it exists only as a matrix, and moving it costs nothing physical.

What happens to the video

The depth map is the enabling ingredient. Once every pixel knows how far away it is, a pixel is no longer a coloured dot on a grid — it is a coloured point in space, and points in space can be photographed from anywhere. The rest is arithmetic:

  1. The four bezel cameras produce a metric depth map — every pixel's distance, in millimetres (§10).
  2. Each pixel and its depth back-project to a 3D point, X = d · K⁻¹ · x̃.
  3. That point projects into the virtual camera at the viewpoint the contract demands, x' = P_offaxis(e) · T · X.
  4. Every pixel lands somewhere new, and near pixels move further than far ones. That is parallax — not applied as an effect, but falling out of the arithmetic.
  5. A z-buffer settles collisions, so the nearer pixel wins.
  6. Whatever gaps remain are filled from a camera that saw behind the edge.

That is the entire manipulation. Each pixel is moved to where its own depth says it belongs from the new viewpoint; nothing is synthesised, only relocated. This is what "warp, don't model" means in practice, and it is the reason every displayed pixel can still be called photographic.

Why it cannot be a flat image operation

It is tempting to imagine this as stretching or shearing the picture. It cannot be. A 2D transform — affine, homography, mesh on the image plane — applies one rule to a whole image or to planar patches, whereas parallax is different pixels moving by different amounts according to their depth. That is exactly the quantity a flat transform has no way to express, and §8 measures the consequence: the best possible single-plane warp recovers 3.4% of the error.

The operation is genuinely three-dimensional, but it maps onto graphics hardware almost perfectly. Lay a mesh grid over the source image, push each vertex along its camera ray by its depth, texture it with the frame, and rasterise — one draw call, with the z-buffer and interpolation free.

Cut the mesh at depth discontinuities

This one detail decides whether the result looks right. Left connected across a silhouette, the triangles spanning the depth jump stretch into rubber sheets and you get smears where you wanted holes. Threshold on depth gradient, drop those triangles, and let the gap be honest — step 6 exists to fill it with real pixels.

What the cameras can and cannot show

A fair objection: if a camera is looking at the top of your arm while you point forward, where do the fronts of your fingers come from? Two different failures hide inside that question and they behave nothing alike.

A surface facing away from all five cameras genuinely cannot be shown. The photons never arrived, and no processing invents them. But a real window has precisely the same limitation — point at your own chest and someone on the far side of the glass sees the back of your hand, not your fingertips. This is not a shortfall against a window; it is the same constraint.

That equivalence is load-bearing. The viewpoint the contract asks for is your eye, beyond the window — only 7° off the blade camera, and comfortably inside the hull of all five (§12). So the surfaces the virtual camera needs are, by construction, the surfaces facing the window: exactly the ones photographed. Fingers pointed forward face the window and every camera sees them. Fingers pointed away are invisible to the far viewer through glass too.

A second, independent reason the blade exists

Hole size scales directly with how far the camera sits off the required viewpoint. A top-bezel webcam opens a 342 mm hole in that same scene — nearly three times as much. Driving alpha down mechanically does not only improve gaze; it shrinks how much of the picture has to be found elsewhere.

Why the result holds together

Nothing here reconstructs a continuous field of rays. It reconstructs one slice — the bundle converging on your eye — and its coherence comes from the depth map. Because every pixel carries a position in space, pixels adjacent on a surface stay adjacent after reprojection. The reconstruction is continuous exactly where the depth is continuous and the surface was seen.

Which says precisely where it breaks: silhouette edges, where depth jumps and a mis-estimated pixel lands far from where it belongs. That is this design's characteristic artefact — never smooth surfaces, always the boundary between a hand and the wall behind it.

How this relates to Gaussian splatting

Closely, for the rendering step; oppositely, for what is being rendered.

Both take 3D points and splat them into a target view, and this forward warp is depth-image-based rendering — the lineage 3DGS descends from, through plenoptic modelling, point splatting and surfels. Step 3 above is one isotropic splat per pixel.

The divergence is total at the level of representation. Gaussian splatting fits a model: gradient descent finds Gaussians whose optimised parameters best reproduce the input photos, so a rendered pixel is a blend of fitted values. This measures and relocates: depth comes from stereo matching, this frame's sensor pixels are moved, and nothing persists to the next frame. 3DGS is a 3D reconstruction — an extremely good-looking one, and on the far side of the line drawn in §20.

One idea worth stealing, one gap worth knowing

Steal: replace point splats with oriented elliptical splats (surfels), using the local depth gradient to give each a normal and an anisotropic footprint so it lands correctly foreshortened. No fitting, no model, no ML — and it attacks the silhouette artefact head-on.

Gap: spherical-harmonic colour gives 3DGS view-dependent appearance, so speculars shift correctly with viewpoint. This warp carries a highlight along with the surface it sits on, leaving it where the source camera saw it. The wettest surface in frame is the cornea — exactly where the design has least room.

Coordinating five cameras

Three separate kinds of coordination, failing in three different ways.

Geometric is solved by a single bundle adjustment with all five seeing the board at once, which fixes their poses relative to each other; the CAD anchor then ties that cluster to the window (§18). Afterwards any camera's pixels can be reprojected into any other's view, because they share one frame.

Temporal is solved adequately rather than well. Cheap UVC modules free-run, so the software reads all five in lockstep from one thread, timestamps on arrival, and rejects any pair skewed by more than 4 ms. Worst case is a full 8 ms frame period, which a hand at 1 m/s turns into 8 mm of depth error. The proper fix is a hardware trigger — one $2 microcontroller driving a square wave into all five pins takes the skew to zero, and it matters more with rolling shutter, where free-running rows drift out of correspondence continuously.

Photometric is mostly handled by identical modules with locked exposure and white balance, with a per-camera 3×3 colour transform fitted off the calibration board to catch the residual (§18). Matching assumes a cheek reads the same in both views, and hole-filling pastes one camera's pixels into another's image, where any mismatch shows as a seam.

All five cameras carry colour

An earlier version of this pipeline treated C as "the picture" and demoted the bezel four to depth and hole-filling — throwing away four live colour images for no reason.

All five should be resampled, weighted by angular proximity to the required viewpoint. C still dominates near frame centre, since at 7° off it needs the least warping and shows the fewest artefacts. But at a silhouette, or inside a disocclusion, a camera that actually saw the surface beats stretching C's edge pixels, and it should win on merit rather than be consulted only once a hole has been declared. Weight sharply, close to winner-take-all — broad averaging across views with imperfect depth ghosts and softens.

A free diagnostic falls out of it

Reproject a point using its depth and sample all five cameras. If they disagree about its colour, the depth is wrong there. That is a direct measurement of the σ_d assumption §22 calls the design's biggest unknown, and it needs no new hardware — two webcams and a face would do it today.

Finding the same point in five images

All of the above assumes a depth map, which assumes knowing which pixel in one camera corresponds to which in another. The premise that makes that tractable is that the perspectives never change: the cameras are bolted and calibrated once, so the correspondence geometry is frozen and precomputed, and only the scene moves.

Given that, epipolar geometry collapses the search to a single row, a census transform makes the comparison robust to sensor-to-sensor differences, and semi-global aggregation resolves what a single patch cannot. §10 works through it in full — including why the baselines are crossed, where σ_d actually comes from, and why the pairs must be fused in depth rather than in disparity.

5 · Pointing, and where it stops

Every pixel is moved by the same rule:

X_world = Tcam→W · ( d(x) · K−1 x̃ )
x′      = Poffaxis(e_peer, W) · X_world

That expression contains no notion of a face, an eye, or a hand. A pointing gesture is a ray from shoulder through fingertip, and rays are preserved by construction. Hands land within a few tenths of a degree, because their error contribution is alpha · dz, and the design drives alpha down to 7°.

Two things still break, and they are named honestly:

  • A hand crossing the window plane has no correct answer. Clip at z = 0.
  • A hand that occludes the face creates a disocclusion the cameras may not see behind.

Portal re-renders through wherever your eye now is, so the gesture keeps landing on the same spot on you and the shift stays at zero — that is the window contract doing the one thing it promises. The webcam's pixels do not know you moved: they stay where they were while you back-project them through an eye that is somewhere else. Raise your eye by a hand's width and its answer slides down by very nearly the same distance; lower your eye and it climbs. Nothing about the far person changed at all — you shifted in your chair.

and the same framing problem bites us

That 34° is not a webcam quirk. It applies to our bezel cameras too, and counting how many of the five carry usable surface near the fingertip gives 2 of 5, and 1 of 5 for some poses. Two is the minimum for stereo. One means the pixels exist but have no depth, so they cannot be reprojected at all — the honest output there is a hole.

It moves with the lens. Pitching all five modules −12° gets 2–3 of 5; a 4:3 sensor at 95° gets 3 of 5 everywhere, at the cost of 27% of the angular resolution, which comes out of the same budget that sets the depth noise. Not yet decided.

Closer than about 250 mm from the glass, nothing helps — the hand has left the camera hull and no lens recovers a view no camera has. So the earlier line about clipping at z = 0 understates it: the pointing volume ends well before the glass.

Since then that depth has actually been computed. The first answer was bad: with depth from the real matcher rather than from the truth, the pointing ray landed 415 to 719 mm from where a window puts it — no better than the webcam it was supposed to beat. With one camera on the fingertip there is nothing to cross-check against, so the matcher rejected 93–98% of it, and the few pixels that survived were the ones it was least able to verify.

What it takes to fix

Three changes, measured one at a time.

The depth search started too far away. The matcher swept from 330 mm to 1050 mm in front of the cameras, sized for a seated head at 578 mm. An extended fingertip is at 270 mm. It was not failing to find the hand — it could not represent it. That is a plain bug, and fixing it alone changes nothing (450 → 441 mm), because the binding constraint is still that one camera sees the finger.

Tilting the cameras down helps, and is not enough. Pitching all five by 12° took one pose from 441 to 309 mm and made another worse: tilting to catch the hand tips the bottom pair off it.

Widening the bottom bar is the change that works. Two more modules at the outer corners — ±280 mm instead of ±125 — puts three cameras on the fingertip instead of one and takes the bottom baseline from 250 mm to 560 mm. Fingertip pixels surviving the matcher go from 1.4–6% to 38–50%.

The local user's eye always has z > 0. The remote person lives at z < 0.

SWAP

SWAP = diag(−1, +1, −1)   # = R_y(180°), det = +1
ELI5

Both ends run identical code. When data arrives from the other end, you spin it 180° about the vertical axis — like turning a chair around to face you. Because spinning twice gets you back where you started, there is no argument about who is "endpoint A". Both sides apply the same transform.

Critically, det(SWAP) = +1. It is a proper rotation, not a reflection, so faces do not come out mirrored.

The mirroring trap — budget a day if you hit it

Raw UVC camera frames are not mirrored. Your right hand appears on the left of the raw image. Applications mirror the preview so it looks like a bathroom mirror. Feed a mirrored preview into solvePnP and you get a left-handed pose, and every downstream sign flips in a way that looks almost right.

7 · Where to put the camera

The camera you actually need sits at the far person's eye — which is behind your screen, and physically unreachable. Your real camera is somewhere else. Decompose that displacement, because the two components cost wildly different amounts.

Axial — along the sight line

Your camera is 570 mm from you; the virtual one is 1150 mm away. This changes perspective compression, so faces look slightly wide-angle.

It costs nothing in gaze. Correcting it is a pull-back along the optical axis, which reveals no new surfaces and therefore produces no holes. Well-conditioned, and optional.

8 · Why no 2D warp can fix this

ELI5

Your eyeball is a ball. The coloured iris sits on the surface of that ball, sticking out slightly toward whatever you're looking at. The corners of your eye are flat on your face.

When you move the camera, the iris shifts relative to the eye corners — because it's at a different depth. A flat image warp slides the whole picture around, so it moves the iris and the corners by the same amount and the difference between them never changes. That difference is the entire gaze signal.

Perceived gaze comes from the iris's offset inside the eye opening. The iris rides on a sphere of radius r ≈ 12 mm. Rotating the viewpoint by alpha moves the iris relative to the eye corners by r·sin(alpha).

A homography is the best possible single-plane warp. It fitted one plane, and both the iris and the eye corners sit in that plane, so it moves them together. It never touches the differential.

How it knows which pixel is which

ELI5

Two eyes see the same face from slightly different places, so everything shifts sideways a little between them — and near things shift more than far things. Measure the shift and you know the distance.

The hard part is deciding which speck in the left picture is the same speck in the right picture. That's the whole game.

The premise that makes it tractable: the perspectives never change. The cameras are bolted down and calibrated once, so the correspondence geometry is frozen and precomputed. Only the scene moves. That's why rigidity is a non-negotiable rather than a nicety.

Epipolar geometry turns a 2D search into a 1D one. A point seen at some pixel in one camera lies along a single ray in space, and that ray projects to a line in the other camera. Rectification warps both images so those lines become the same horizontal row — computed once, applied per frame as a lookup. Matching is then: scan one row. Disparity d is the shift along it, and z = f·b/d.

"Best match" is not brightness. That would assume two sensors report the same value for the same cheek, which is exactly what auto-exposure and sensor scatter destroy. SGBM matches on a census transform: each pixel becomes a bit-string encoding "is each neighbour brighter than me?", compared by Hamming distance. It depends only on local ordering, so it survives gain and tint differences. Census is what makes cheap sensors viable at all.

One pixel is never enough. Smooth forehead matches equally well everywhere along the row — the aperture problem. Match a small window rather than a point, then aggregate cost semi-globally along about eight paths across the image with a smoothness penalty, so a confident match at an edge propagates into the ambiguous region beside it. The "SG" does most of the work.

Why the baselines are crossed — an information argument

A horizontal pair searches along horizontal epipolar lines, so a purely horizontal edge slides along its own search line at zero cost and is unmatchable. Eyelids and shoulders are horizontal edges. A vertical pair nails them exactly. This is a stronger reason for the rectangle than viewpoint coverage.

Where σ_d actually comes from. Fit a parabola through the cost at d−1, d, d+1 and take its minimum. On textured surfaces the trough is sharp and you interpolate to 0.1–0.3 px. On a dark iris carrying a specular highlight the curve is flat or multi-modal, and σ_d degrades precisely where the design has least room. This is why the bias light is mandatory — not for exposure, but to put texture on skin.

Fuse the pairs in depth, not in disparity

With four bezel cameras, build a single cost volume indexed by depth and sum across all pairs. A true depth is consistent in every pair simultaneously; a false match is consistent only in the pair that produced it, so summing suppresses it. Computing three separate disparity maps and averaging them discards that entirely.

Left–right consistency finds occlusions. Match L→R and R→L; where they disagree the surface is visible to one camera only. Those become the flagged holes, filled from whichever camera saw behind the edge. And what none of it fixes: a genuinely textureless region carries no information, so SGBM interpolates from the edges around it — a well-behaved guess, but a guess.

What happened when it was actually built

All of the above is the design. Writing it and running the whole pipeline on what it produced — rather than on depth handed over by the simulator — turned up four things the design did not anticipate.

census cannot give sub-pixel depth

A census code is read at a whole pixel, so the matching cost is a staircase in sub-pixel shift, and the parabola that is supposed to interpolate the trough fits the stairs instead. Measured, that alone costs an order of magnitude in sigma_d. The fix is a split: census for the coarse search, where its immunity to gain and tint is exactly what five mismatched sensors need, and a continuous, locally normalised correlation for the final sub-pixel step.

sigma_d is about 0.5 px, not the 0.3 assumed throughout. Measured against known truth at 1920×1080 on skin with 4% local contrast. The design-check margin goes from 5.5× to about 3.3×. It still closes — with less room than advertised.

The median is not the problem; the tail is. Half a pixel of noise is a fifth of a millimetre of depth. But the 99th percentile reaches 9 mm on the face and 300 mm on the outer cameras. Those are blunders, not noise, and every local confidence test passes them. Only checking one camera's answer against another's removes them, which is why that step is not optional.

Depth error at an eye opening is systematic, and the error budget treats it as noise. A matcher that correlates a small window biases depth wherever that window straddles a step — and an eye opening is a step. The iris-to-corner depth differential comes out a steady 0.4 mm. Being steady, it does not average away over a thousand iris pixels, and alpha · dz converts it straight into a gaze bias. Trying to detect and discard the affected pixels throws away 69% of the eye region and makes everything worse.

and one that was nearly a disaster

The rule for combining the five reprojected images let a later camera claim a pixel if it was 1 mm nearer. With perfect depth that is harmless. With real depth it is close to the worst rule available: noise is a few tenths of a millimetre, so the winner is often just whichever camera erred toward the viewer — and the ones most likely to win are the outer ones, whose alpha is largest, which turn that error into the largest sideways shift. Occlusions are centimetre-scale. Noise is sub-millimetre. Put the threshold between them.

  • Scale ambiguity and per-user IPD calibration both disappear. Depth arrives in metres. A monocular design has to guess absolute scale from the distance between someone's pupils, and be recalibrated per person.
  • Nothing in the depth path knows what a face is. Hands, objects, hair and glasses all get depth on the same terms as eyes.

13 · Motion parallax

When you move your head sideways by Δ, the correct image is the one a camera displaced by Δ would have captured. To first order that is a screen-space shift:

shift = Δ · (1 − z_screen / z_virtual)

where z_screen is your distance to your own screen, and z_virtual is the distance from your eye to the thing being drawn.

The trap: this constant is not the implementation

For the peer's face at 1.15 m the gain is 0.478. It is tempting to apply 0.478 to the whole frame. Don't. The gain is a function of each pixel's own depth, and the difference lands squarely on pointing — which is half the point of the system.

15 · The cancellation — the best result in the design

ELI5

Your computer guesses where your eyes are. It gets it wrong by a centimetre. You'd think that ruins everything.

It doesn't — as long as it tells the same lie twice. If it uses the same wrong guess both to draw your friend on your screen and to tell your friend's computer where you are, then: your screen draws them in the wrong place, you look at the wrong pixel — and their view was rendered from that same wrong place, so your gaze lands exactly right.

Two wrongs make a right, and they make it exactly right.

Measured: 0.00° at 40 mm of tracking error. Not "small". Zero.

But be precise about what that claims

This page has also said differential gaze degrades as Δ / 1.15 m — about 0.5° per centimetre, which is 2.0° at that same 40 mm. Both numbers are right. They measure different things, and running them together understates what head tracking has to achieve.

A consistent estimate is a change of coordinates, not a distortion. Feed one value to both the packet and the render and the scene drawn is exactly right for that value — no ray bent, no angle corrupted. That is what the 0.00° measures, and it is worth protecting.

But consistency does not move your head. Your eye is still Δ away from the seat the picture was drawn for, and on a flat panel that residual is the Mona Lisa case. Eye contact survives it. The left-eye/right-eye differential does not, because that is exactly a small angular distinction referenced to where your eyes actually are.

The error budget in §14 is missing this term

Its pessimistic stack scores 10 mm of peer tracking under the first column — 0.00° — and totals 0.55°. Scored honestly that same 10 mm contributes 0.50°, pushing the stack to 0.74° in quadrature or 1.05° if the terms add.

So head tracking accuracy is not free, and the design of record implies it is. The real requirement is roughly 10 mm absolute. That does not break anything — 10 mm from stereo-triangulated landmarks at 600 mm is very achievable — but it has to be budgeted rather than assumed away. At 20 mm the design fails on any pessimistic combination.

Consistency buys an undistorted scene. It does not buy the right seat.

The condition, and it is fragile

This holds if and only if one estimate, stamped once per frame, feeds both the outgoing position packet and the local render. It breaks the moment the render thread and the network thread read estimates from different frames — and then 10 mm costs 0.50°.

The Mona Lisa effect, working for you

A flat image of a face gazing into the lens reads as gazing at you from any viewing angle. Two consequences, one good and one to respect:

  • Eye contact survives viewer movement and a wrong declared position.
  • Differential gaze does not. Whether they are looking at your left eye or your right degrades as Δ / 1.15 m — about 0.5° per centimetre of drift.

Why it warps twice

Read cold, the pipeline looks like it does the same work at both ends. It does not, and the split is deliberate.

The sender performs the large viewpoint change — from the rig to the peer's last-known eye position — because that is the only place where five real cameras exist to fill the holes it opens. The receiver corrects only for however far the head moved during one network round trip.

All five cameras are colour sources

An earlier version of this pipeline treated C as "the picture" and demoted the bezel four to depth and hole-filling, which throws away four live colour images.

Resample all five, weighted by angular proximity to the required viewpoint. C still dominates near frame centre — at 7° off it needs the least warping — but at a silhouette or in a disocclusion, a camera that actually saw the surface beats stretching C's edge pixels, and it should win on merit rather than be consulted only after a hole is declared. Weight sharply, close to winner-take-all: broad averaging across views with imperfect depth ghosts and softens.

A free diagnostic falls out of it

Reproject a point using its depth and sample all five cameras. If they disagree on its colour, the depth is wrong there. That is a direct measurement of the σ_d assumption §22 calls the design's biggest unknown, and it needs no new hardware.

Head tracking must not ride on the dense depth map

It sits inside the motion-to-photon loop and the depth solve does not. Triangulate a handful of landmarks across the bezel pair and keep that path cheap and separate — otherwise you have put the slowest stage inside the fastest loop.

Use that single value for both the outgoing packet and the local render. This is what makes tracking error cancel exactly. Separate reads on separate threads break it and cost 0.50° per 10 mm.
Warp, do not model
Every output pixel is a resampled input pixel. Depth decides where pixels move, nothing more. Do not build a textured face mesh — it substitutes a model for the video and looks like a mask.
Render locally
Head tracking is local, so network latency becomes conversational lag with zero geometric error. Motion-to-photon under 50 ms.
Never scale to fit
If the two monitors differ, both ends letterbox to the largest rectangle that fits in both, in millimetres. Scaling preserves the picture and destroys the angles, and the angles are the entire point.
Frame sync
Five unsynchronised UVC streams carry up to 8 ms of skew, which a hand at 1 m/s turns into 8 mm of depth error. Read all five in lockstep from one thread, timestamp on arrival, and reject any pair worse than 4 ms — falling back to the previous depth map rather than trusting it.
Split the USB topology
Five 1080p60 MJPEG streams run about 17 MB/s. USB 3 handles the throughput, but UVC bandwidth negotiation fails well before the bus limit. Two hubs, on separate host controllers.
Degrade gracefully
If tracking drops out, fall back to the last known eye position, never to a default. The call degrades to a fixed viewpoint instead of lurching.

18 · Calibration

Prerequisites — skipped more often than any other step

Identify cameras by physical port path. Cheap modules share one VID/PID with no serial, so /dev/video* reshuffles on reboot. Key on /dev/v4l/by-path/ and label every cable.

Lock every camera. Disable autofocus, auto-exposure and auto white balance; pin fixed values; use the same exposure and white balance on all five.

Why identical exposure is not fussiness

Stereo matching assumes a pixel of a cheek has the same brightness in both views. Auto-exposure is precisely what destroys that assumption. Autofocus that hunts mid-call silently changes your intrinsics and ruins depth with no visible symptom until gaze is wrong.

Three stages

  1. Intrinsics, per camera. Checkerboard, 20 poses, covering the frame edges where distortion lives. Expect RMS under 0.4 px.
  2. Relative extrinsics, all five simultaneously. One board where the head normally sits, visible to all five at once, 30 poses. Expect a bundle residual under 0.5 px.
  3. Anchor to the screen, from CAD. Non-interactive: rig geometry plus three ruler measurements.

Photometric alignment, per camera

Identical modules still scatter in sensor gain and tint. Matching assumes a cheek reads the same in both views, and hole-filling pastes one camera's pixels into another's image, where a mismatch shows as a seam. While the board is up, capture one frame per camera of a flat evenly-lit target and fit a 3×3 colour matrix plus offset against the median of all five. Apply on ingest, before rectification. This does not replace locking exposure and white balance — it corrects the residual those cannot reach.

You cannot calibrate against the screen surface

An early version of this design proposed taping the board flat to the monitor and treating the screen as the calibration plane. That is invalid for this layout: the bezel cameras are mounted in the screen plane and point outward, so they see it edge-on and the pose is degenerate. CAD plus three ruler measurements is good to about 1 mm against a 10 mm tolerance.

19 · Verification — how you know it works

The build's key diagnostic was originally stated as: render a virtual cube in the window plane; if it stays welded to the glass, calibration and head tracking are both right.

That turns out to be wrong in an interesting way.

A point at z = 0 is invariant to eye position

Work the projection through for a point in the window plane:

A drawing on the glass itself doesn't move when you move your head — because it's on the glass. So a test target drawn in the window plane can never reveal a head-tracking bug. It literally cannot see it.

So the test is blind to one of the two suspects it was supposed to distinguish. It has to become two tests.

A centred rectangle is symmetric under x → −x. The formal acceptance test

Tape a numbered 5×3 grid to the wall behind each monitor and mark the far person's left eye, right eye and mouth. Thirty trials each, blind: they fixate a called target, you report what you perceived.

  • Coarse pass, 4°: face vs hands vs away — 90% correct.
  • Fine pass, 1°: left eye vs right eye vs mouth — 80% correct.
  • Pointing pass: they point at one of five marks on your torso and desk; you identify which — 90% correct.

Run the same protocol over an ordinary video call as a control. Have the number, not the impression.

20 · Dead ends, and why each died

Each of these was seriously considered. They are recorded so nobody re-derives them.

ML gaze redirection dead

Snaps gaze toward the camera. Manufactures constant eye contact and destroys the differential signal. Wrong tool for this goal, not merely imperfect.

Textured face-mesh proxy dead

Replaces the video with a model: looks like a video-game character wearing your friend's face, and hands leaving the head-and-shoulders box smear. Killed by the requirement "I want to see the person I'm calling."

Generic monocular depth dead

Scale-ambiguous, temporally flickery, and weakest exactly on the eye region. Superseded by stereo. Note the irony: depth turned out to be the most forgiving term in the budget, so monocular's headline weakness was never the real problem.

Pure 2D homography dead

Recovers 3.4% of the gaze error. Structurally incapable, not just inaccurate.

Bezel-only camera placement dead

Needs 0.66 mm corneal depth accuracy — eyeball radius alone varies ±0.5 mm between people. And even with stereo beating that on paper, the eyelid occlusion bias at 18° remains: viewed from above, the upper lid covers more of the iris than it does head-on. That is an occlusion, not a displacement, and a depth-driven warp has no pixels to put there.

This is why the blade exists

Stereo depth is good enough on paper for a bezel-only rig. The blade is there because at alpha = 7° the eyelid difference is negligible, and at 18° it is a systematic bias no depth map can fix.

Beamsplitter / teleprompter fold back pocket

Optically the best answer. Camera at the conjugate point, raw video, zero warping, exact gaze and pointing. Genuinely commodity — teleprompter glass is $40–90. The "glassy" look is fixable with proper wedged glass plus matte black flocking.

Rejected on bulk: about 90 cm of desk depth for a 27″ panel, or 40 cm plus a folded side-arm using a mirror. Also costs ~50% of screen brightness.

Worth keeping in mind for a reason separate from the one it was rejected on: it removes the entire depth pipeline, so it is also the cheapest possible way to test whether the premise is worth building before committing to the compact version.

Pinhole in a rear-projection screen if requirements change

Same optical trick with no glass in the sight line: UST projector, screen with a 6 mm hole, camera 550 mm behind it. Full body at 80–100″. About $800 and needs a dim room. The best option if standing and full-body gesture ever become requirements.

Light-field displays, shape-matched displays, eyeball actuators out of budget

All correct, all far outside budget. One notable piece of theory: a diffuse display can never serve multiple viewers, but a display surface that coincides with the subject's true surface needs no angular emission at all, because skin is roughly Lambertian. Head tracking and angular bandwidth are both compensations for a display surface sitting in the wrong place.

Three- and four-camera layouts dead

Coverage fails. See §12.

Screen surface as the calibration plane dead

Proposed early, invalid for the final layout: cameras mounted in the screen plane pointing outward see that plane edge-on.

A fixed chair superseded

Replaced by head tracking. The residual is much weaker: drop tracking entirely and you need a measured seating position with about ±2 cm of slack, constraining only the viewer and requiring no alignment to the camera.

21 · Known limits

The declared blind spots

These five are decisions, not bugs. Each is a volume or a case the design does not serve, chosen because the alternatives cost more than the case is worth. The right thing to do with them is put them in the product, not discover them mid-call.

And the rest

  • The pointing volume ends about 150 mm from the glass — see the blind-spot table above. With the five bezel modules alone an extended fingertip was framed by one or two of them and could not be reprojected at all; the four aimed near-group modules fix that down to about 150 mm and no further. A hand crossing the window plane is undefined outright, so clip at z = 0.
  • Pointing does not survive a real matcher. With depth actually computed from the pixels rather than handed over by the simulator, the pointing ray lands 415–719 mm off — because 93–98% of the fingertip is rejected for want of a second camera that can see it. Gaze survives at 0.55–0.89° against a 1.00° bar. Pointing does not survive at all, and the fix is optical, not algorithmic.
  • sigma_d is 0.5 px measured, against 0.3 px assumed. Design-check margin 3.3× rather than 5.5×. And the depth error near an eye opening is systematic rather than random, which the budget adds in quadrature as though it averaged out.
  • The matcher keeps 44–72% of the face and 39–41% of an extended hand. Everything else is a hole, to be filled from a neighbouring camera or inpainted.
  • Fast hand movement shows sync artefacts. Faces do not.
  • Head turns past roughly 60° leave usable stereo coverage.
  • Viewer travel beyond ±250 mm leaves the camera hull and the overscan margin at once.
  • 1:1 scale makes the far face smaller than video calls have trained people to expect. Anyone judging on picture size will call it a downgrade.
  • Both ends need a rig for reciprocal gaze. One-sided installs still give one-directional gaze and are worth testing.
  • Glasses shift the apparent iris by a prescription-dependent amount. Not corrected — measure it during acceptance testing instead of guessing.
  • Two people at one end breaks it. The render serves one tracked viewer.
  • The render is monoscopic, and focus sits at the screen. One image is drawn from one tracked eye, so both of your eyes see the same picture. Direction and angular size are exact at every depth — verified to machine precision — but the light physically leaves the panel at 600 mm while the far person is meant to be at 1150 mm. Accommodation and vergence therefore sit at the screen while parallax says otherwise, and binocular disparity is absent. Gaze survives (the iris cue is monocular) and so does pointing; what it costs is presence. This is a display limit, not a capture one — the beamsplitter does not fix it either.
  • 20% overscan leaves about 1370 px across the window, or 2.3 arcmin per pixel at 600 mm. Adequate, not sharp.
  • Depth is weakest on the eye region, which is where the design is tightest. Mitigated by low alpha, not solved.

22 · Where this might actually fail

The limits above are the ones the design admits. These are the ones worth watching, ranked by what would most likely kill the project rather than by how embarrassing it is. Everything here is untested.

1 · the matcher may not run at frame rate, and nobody has checked

The largest unexamined risk in the design, and it is not an optical one. The plane sweep evaluates, per reference camera per frame, 1920×1080 pixels × 96 depth planes × up to eight partners. In numpy on two cores that took about ten minutes per frame. Frame rate needs 16 ms. That is four orders of magnitude, and the honest reductions — three reference cameras instead of nine, 720p, 64 planes — buy maybe two of them.

Two consequences the bill of materials does not carry. The "$200, runs on a cheap single-board computer" framing is almost certainly dead; this wants a discrete GPU. And every accuracy figure above was measured at settings that may not survive the cut, because resolution and plane count are exactly what sigma_d is made of.

2 · Everything is measured on a synthetic head

Spheres with a procedural albedo. No hair, no eyebrows, no stubble, no glasses, no clothing, no specular highlights, no motion, no rolling shutter, no compression. The texture is smoother than a real face in some places, which is pessimistic, and cleaner in others, which is not. sigma_d = 0.5 px is a synthetic number, and the eye region — dark irises, low texture, and speculars that move with viewpoint and violate brightness constancy outright — is exactly where a synthetic model is least trustworthy. It is measurable in an afternoon with two webcams and a face.

3 · the gaze budget probably does not close once head tracking is counted

The measured 0.55–0.89° was taken with the viewer's eye position known exactly. Head tracking's wrong-seat term at the stated 10 mm requirement is 0.50°, and it is a different error from the ones already inside that figure. In quadrature:

face compositemeasured+ 10 mm trackingvs 1.00° bar
five cameras, 10 mm z-margin0.89°1.02°fails
centre camera only, holes filled0.55°0.74°passes

So the five-camera composite fails and the centre camera alone passes — the opposite of what "more cameras is better" suggests. Either head tracking has to beat 10 mm, or the face composite has to be centre-dominant. That is a decision to make deliberately, not one to let the first code written settle.

4 · Two subsystems meet at a seam nobody has rendered

The face group and the near group overlap on the torso and upper arm. They are independent solves, they will disagree at the millimetre level, and the compositing rule picks a winner per pixel per frame. That is a recipe for temporal shimmer along the seam even when both answers are individually fine. Cheap to test, and untested.

5 · Nine free-running camera streams

The sync analysis was written for five. Nine 1080p60 MJPEG streams are about 30 MB/s across two hubs, and the 8 ms worst-case skew now applies to more pairs. The $3 trigger microcontroller has gone from a nicety to nearly mandatory.

6 · Calibration got materially harder

Nine cameras, two lens types, full per-camera rotation rather than position on a plane — and the awkward part, the near group is aimed at a volume the face group barely sees, so one checkerboard sequence may not pin down the relative pose between the groups. That inter-group extrinsic is exactly what makes a pointing ray land in the same frame as the shoulder it starts from.

7 · Rigidity, over a bigger frame

The L³ stiffness analysis covered a bezel bar. The rig is now a perimeter frame with 42 mm of overhang each side on a 27-inch, carrying nine modules, quite possibly screwed to a television's flexing plastic. More modules is more drift paths, and drift is extrinsic error is exactly what the calibration chain exists to prevent.

8 · The blunder tail

Median depth error is 0.2 mm; the 99th percentile reaches 300 mm on the outer cameras. The cross-check removes most. What survives is a fragment of surface placed somewhere it isn't — a speckle in a still frame, a flicker in motion, and the eye is very good at noticing flicker.

9 · There is a rigorous error budget and no latency budget

"Motion-to-photon under 50 ms" appears as an assertion. Head-tracking latency produces the same visible failure as a calibration error, and flaw 1 says the depth stage is nowhere near a 50 ms operation. A design this careful about angles should have a budget for milliseconds.

10 · The gaze measurement is a proxy

Iris centroid minus corner centroid, in millimetres on the glass, converted to degrees. A reasonable stand-in for what a human reads gaze from, and not a human. The blind trial in §19 is what settles it, and no simulation replaces it.

11 · You learn nothing until nearly everything is built

The design makes two independent claims — that restoring geometry restores gaze, and that a 1:1 head-tracked window creates presence and readable pointing. The build order validates neither until nine cameras, a calibration chain, a depth pipeline and a warp shader all exist.

Both are separately testable for under $100. The window-contract claim needs one ordinary webcam: accept the 18° gaze error, build the off-axis render and head tracking, and find out whether 1:1 scale reads as an aperture or as a small picture — which §21 already flags as a real risk to the entire premise.
